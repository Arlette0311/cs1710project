1 Growing up

First, children talk more as they grow

Each dot is one child's recorded day. The black line follows the typical (median) child at each age. A baby around 6 months makes about 90 speech-like sounds an hour. By age 3 it is closer to 200.

So every comparison in this story is made between children of similar ages. A 4-year-old out-talking a baby tells us nothing about their homes.

Look at the spread: at every age, some children make two or three times more sounds than others. What goes along with that difference?

2 The core relationship

Children who hear more adult talk say more themselves

Across all recordings, more adult talk goes with more child speech (correlation r = 0.30). On average, each extra 100 adult vocalizations an hour goes with about 31 more from the child.

3 The echo

The gap between quiet and talkative homes widens with age

Each band is drawn like a sound wave, made of real recordings. Within an age group, recordings are lined up from least to most adult talk. Above the line is the adult talk the child heard. Below the line is the child's own speech, its "echo".

For babies the orange echo stays almost flat. By ages 3–4 it swells on the right: children in the most talkative fifth of homes make about 3.3× as many speech sounds as those in the quietest fifth.

4 Diminishing returns?

Babies level off. Preschoolers keep climbing.

Here each age group's recordings are split into five equal groups by adult talk. The steps show the typical child's speech in each fifth.

For babies under 1, child speech barely changes after the second step. For 3- to 4-year-olds there is no plateau: each step up in adult talk comes with more child speech.

More talk doesn't stop mattering. Its link with a child's own speech grows as children start to use language themselves.

5 Same child, different days

Even the same child tends to talk more on high-talk days

Maybe chatty families just have chatty kids. To check, we compare children with themselves: many were recorded on several days. Each line connects one child's quietest recorded day (least adult talk) to their busiest day (most adult talk), recorded close together in age.

Across all 335 children with repeat recordings, the within-child correlation is r = 0.22, about 22 more child sounds per 100 more adult sounds. That is smaller than across children, but it's there. The day-to-day link is real, though noisy.

6 What doesn't explain it

Family background is a weaker, less consistent clue

At first glance, some groups look ahead. Children of mothers without a high-school diploma make the most speech sounds. But that group is older on average, and most of its recordings come from a single research team.

Flip the switch to compare children of the same age, hearing the same amount of adult talk, in the same research team. The gaps shrink toward zero.

The message

Across very different families and countries, the talk around a child tracks the talk from the child, and the link grows as they grow.

Mother's education, gender and multilingualism are much less consistent clues. This is an observational pattern, so we do not claim that adding a set amount of talk produces a set amount of child speech.

Takeaway

What this suggests for parents, caregivers and educators

These are encouragements, not prescriptions. The study shows a pattern, not a recipe, and no one should feel blamed by it.

Make room for conversation

Narrate, ask, wait, respond. Everyday back-and-forth is something any household can do, whatever its background.

Keep it up as children grow

The link between talk heard and talk produced is strongest for 3- and 4-year-olds, so talking with older toddlers still matters.

Don't read destiny into demographics

Multilingual homes and less-schooled parents were not behind once age and talk were compared fairly. Avoid deficit assumptions.
